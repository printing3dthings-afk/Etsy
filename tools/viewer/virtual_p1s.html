What this is

Every path on screen came out of a real slicer, run against the real STL in this shop's openscad_models/ tree. Nothing here is a drawing of a print — it is the print instructions, drawn.

A clean replay here is NOT a clean print

Nothing on this page is physics. No gravity, no molten plastic, no heat, no cooling, no bed adhesion, no layer bonding, no moisture. The Material tab changes text on this screen and nothing about the toolpath — the same G-code prints in PLA or PETG. A part can replay perfectly here and still fail on the plate, and the failures this cannot see (warping, a curled corner the nozzle then hits, delamination, stringing) are the ones that actually ruin prints.
Predicting warping for real means thermo-mechanical FEA — a different class of tool entirely. What is honest to do is measure signals from the real file that correlate with known failures: the longest genuinely unsupported span, layers too quick to cool, the area gripping the plate, the tall-and-narrow ratio. That is tools/print_risk.py, and it reports numbers, not verdicts — its thresholds stay guesses until real prints are logged against them with --record.
Worked example of why a number needs care. The longest bridge MOVE on the sauce tray is 107.9 mm, which sounds unprintable. The longest genuinely unsupported run inside that move is 5.6 mm — the rest passes over ground already printed. It sits at z=0.8 mm: the ceiling of the engraved maker's mark on the underside. The scary number was the wrong measurement.

What it is not

This is PrusaSlicer, not Bambu Studio. They share an engine lineage — Bambu Studio and Orca are both PrusaSlicer forks — so perimeters, overhang classification, bridges and support generation track closely. Speeds and time estimates will not match what your P1S shows. Treat the clock as a ratio between jobs, not a promise.
Real print mode hides things that are really there. Sparse infill and the skirt are switched off so you see the part the way you would with the door open — they are still printed, still filament, still time on the clock. Both wall loops and the solid layers stay on, and supports stay on, because they are physically standing on the plate until you snap them off. Layer lines are drawn at the plate's own measured layer pitch and fade out at zoom levels where a camera could not resolve them either. Each bead is a closed tube, so the dark speckle that used to show up along curved walls (you were seeing straight into the hollow underside of a bead) is gone. What is still a simplification is the bead's cross-section: four points, not a real half-cylinder. Measured against macro photographs of real printed walls, the ridge between layers here comes out at 6.8% of local brightness against 13.7–20.7% in the photographs — so layer lines read softer here than they do on the actual part.
Part only shows you less than the plate really holds, on purpose. Real-print mode answers "what is on the plate right now", so it keeps supports — they are genuinely printed plastic standing there until you snap them off. Part only answers a different question: what the finished object looks like in your hand. So supports, their interface, the skirt and sparse infill are all switched off. That is not a small edit on some plates: on the fairy house, support material plus its interface is 44.3% of every extrusion move on the plate. It is also lit by its own two-light rig rather than the chamber, because hiding the enclosure hides the chamber lamp with it — including a light from below, which no real P1S has, so that the first layer is readable at all.
The lighting is a studio rig, not this machine's chamber. Since 2026-09-17 the scene renders with physically based materials, an environment map and real shadows, which is a large step up in how it looks and no step at all in what it knows. The key light, the softbox behind it and the floor are invented to make the shape readable; the P1S's actual LED is one bar on the left beam. A prettier replay is not a more accurate one, and the closer this gets to looking photographic the more worth saying that is.
Nothing thermal is modelled. Warping, bed adhesion, stringing, layer delamination and heat creep are invisible here. A part can replay perfectly and still fail on the plate for a reason this cannot see.
Travel moves are not drawn. They are roughly half the move count and none of them lay plastic, so they are dropped from the payload. The nozzle marker jumps between paths the way the real head does, but the flight path is not shown.
A layer count is layer changes, not height divided by layer height. On any plate with supports the two differ, because the slicer gives support material its own layer heights and injects extra layer changes between the object's. Measured: this pumpkin at 0.20 mm is 469 uniform layers with supports off and 604 with them on, same 93.8 mm part. The number shown is the real count of layer changes the printer performs.
Coordinates are rounded to 0.01 mm. That is a fortieth of a 0.42 mm bead — below anything this view can resolve — and it is what makes an 830,000-move plate fit in a web page.

Profile used

Stock 0.4 mm brass nozzle, 0.20 mm layers, 2 perimeters, 15% grid infill, supports on at a 45° threshold, PLA at 220 °C on a 55 °C textured PEI plate. That is the stock Standard profile, not Scott's tuned ones — swap those in at tools/virtual_printer.py and re-export to replay them here.

Rebuilding this

tools/virtual_printer.py slices a model. tools/gcode_viewer_data.py turns that G-code into the payload this page replays. Adding a job is two commands, not a code change.
