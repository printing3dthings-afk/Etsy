# Draw Characters That Survive the Nozzle

The fastest way for this shop to make better printed characters is a fixed order of work with a measurable check at every stage. A new modelling tool will not do it. The order: pick one line of action and a numeric proportion system, block out a few big masses, prove the pose as a solid black silhouette, open negative space so no two parts merely touch, and only then smooth the outline and add the small detail a 0.4 mm nozzle can actually keep. Every defect in the Cupid rounds maps to a well-documented principle that was skipped. The clutter is detail added before form. The backwards legs break the knee's one-way hinge. The bow touching the face is a tangent. The bulging back, which the fix made worse, is a proportion that was nudged by eye instead of measured against a fixed reference. For stylized pieces, chibi proportions of 2.5–3 heads tall and the experimentally tested "baby schema" features carry the appeal. For realistic pieces, a 7–7.5-head canon with miniature-style exaggeration keeps faces legible. But at 60 mm tall a facial third is only about 2 mm, so only the big planes survive. In code, OpenSCAD 2D work is the best route for charms: a morphological closing plus BOSL2 `smooth_path`, which cut the worst crease from 80° to about 5° in a local test. A numpy signed-distance model with smooth-min is the best route for chubby figurines, and realistic faces remain beyond scripted modelling. The shop's own measured limits in `DETAIL_STANDARD.md` override generic web figures, which disagree with each other by about 2×. On the legal side, study many references and draw new forms. Tracing or height-mapping one copyrighted image makes a derivative work. Etsy's original-design rule is real, but the date CLAUDE.md gives for it needs checking.

## Four Cupid defects trace to four skipped principles

**Clutter is detail placed before form.** Every practitioner source gives the same order: concept, turnaround, primary masses kept "as long as possible", then secondary forms, then tertiary detail last ([80 Level: Patrick Yeung](https://80.lv/articles/stylized-character-production-tips-and-tricks)). The Disney principle of appeal asks for a balance: not so simple it bores, and not so complex the viewer cannot follow it ([SIGGRAPH HyperGraph: Appeal](https://education.siggraph.org/static/HyperGraph/animation/character_animation/principles/appeal.htm)). The Polycount critique rule is blunter, **"say it, or don't"**: any form you include needs a strong presence in the silhouette, and geometry that adds nothing to it should go ([Polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/comment/2790513)). Chibi guides say the same: remove any detail that does not add to the kawaii factor ([Clip Studio](https://tips.clip-studio.com/zh-tw/articles/4973?org=1)). A flat white charm makes this stricter. There is no colour to separate regions, so every interior mark must be either an engraved groove at least 0.4 mm wide or a raised edge. Feather tips and curls finer than the shop's 0.8 mm island floor are dropped by the slicer, not shrunk (`DETAIL_STANDARD.md` rule 11). The practical move is to merge each wing into three to five large scalloped lobes instead of drawing individual feathers.

**Backwards legs break a one-way hinge.** "The leg is not a straight line." The lower leg is offset from the upper, and the knee bends one way and stops at a set point in the other ([Artists Network](https://www.artistsnetwork.com/art-mediums/drawing/common-mistakes-of-drawing-the-leg)). For a chubby cherub leg, that cited rule combines with two others into a construction. Baby limbs have minimal joint definition ([DrawingHowToDraw](https://www.drawinghowtodraw.com/drawing-lessons/drawing-faces-lessons/articles/proportions-children-figures.html)), so draw the thigh as a teardrop, wider at the hip, with a crease rather than a bony knee. Each limb should set a straight side against a curved one ([Animator Island](https://www.animatorisland.com/perfect-posing-2-appealing-and-readable/)), so keep the shin front nearly straight and put the calf bulge on the back. The foot's toes point the same way as the knee. A leg reads as backwards when the calf bulge sits on the front, or when the foot points away from the knee's side. That gives a check a script can run on the outline: the knee vertex must lie in front of the hip–ankle line, the calf bulge behind it, and the toe on the knee's side. Animal hind legs are a separate trap. Their backward-pointing joint is the hock (the ankle), not a knee, and the leg should form a "Z" ([Drawabox, via search summary](https://www.drawabox.com/lesson/5/2)).

**A bow touching the face is a tangent.** Tangents are outlines of separate objects meeting at a single point. They flatten depth and muddy the read ([Animator Island](https://www.animatorisland.com/perfect-posing-2-appealing-and-readable/)). John Baldessari recalled a teacher's rule: elements should either overlap or have space between them, and near-touching "kissing" creates discomfort ([Gurney Journey](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2010/11/tangencies-in-real-life.html?m=1)). On a printed charm a tangent is also a manufacturing defect. A near-touch becomes either a sliver of plastic too thin to survive handling (the shop wants 1.2 mm, three beads, for anything handled; `DETAIL_STANDARD.md` rule 5) or a slot so narrow the slicer fills it, since an 0.8 mm slot can close up depending on extrusion width and perimeter count ([LayerCraftLog](https://layercraftlog.com/fdm-printing/how-does-extrusion-width-relate-to-nozzle-size-and-print-resolution)). The fix is to hold the bow out in front of the body, so its arc and string make a clear negative-space "D" of their own. Then either overlap the bow decisively with the hand or separate it from the face by a gap that reads at thumbnail size.

**The bulging back, and the fix that worsened it, come from changing shape without measuring.** Feedback professionals treat a circled note as a symptom. They name the principle behind it, change one isolated thing, and show before and after at the identical angle ([CGMA](https://cgmasteracademy.com/blog/how-to-read-minds-and-decode-artistic-feedback.html)). The chibi torso is a bean or pear with its weight low and no real shoulders ([Clip Studio](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/4863)). Read with "straight against curve", that points to a flat back set against a round belly front, the reverse of what the drafts did.

There is also a code-specific reason a smoothing fix can grow a bulge. OpenSCAD's `hull()`, `minkowski()` and morphological closing can only add material. Hull and minkowski round convex shapes and fill concavities. Closing fills concave creases but cannot pull a convex bump back in. So "smoothing" a lumpy back with any of them enlarges it. That consequence is this report's inference from the operators' documented behaviour ([OpenSCAD manual: offset](https://en.wikibooks.org/wiki/OpenSCAD_User_Manual/Transformations#offset)). A hand retrace drifts the same way. In a local test a hand-placed 10-point spline over a blocked-out head scored only **IoU 0.831 and a Hausdorff distance of 7.96 mm** against the shape it was meant to follow, while a closing scored IoU 0.997 and 0.59 mm. Unless the new outline is compared against the approved one with numbers, nobody notices the drift.

## Chibi at 2.5–3 heads, with every joint tilted, passes the black-fill test

### Construction order and shape language

Preston Blair's construction sequence starts with a line of action through the figure. Rounded masses for head and body go on next, then details "fitted or hinged into position", then cleanup; he calls proportion one of the most important factors ([Blair, via secondary excerpt](https://jamshidnoori.blogspot.com/p/animation.html)). In code this means one C- or S-shaped spline first. Then head, torso and pelvis ellipses along it, each rotated to the curve's local tangent so no two share an axis. Limbs and props come last.

Three posing rules turn directly into geometry. Give "a different angle for every joint", and tilt the shoulders, hips and head that beginners leave level ([Animator Island](https://www.animatorisland.com/perfect-posing-2-appealing-and-readable/)); for a Cupid, that means the near wing larger and higher than the far one, and the bow arm and string arm at clearly different angles. Avoid twinning, the unintended mirroring of both sides that Thomas and Johnston showed has a "killing effect" ([Animator Island: Twinning](https://www.animatorisland.com/defining-the-art-twinning/)); the 3/4 view "doesn't offer many opportunities for twinning" ([Character Design Notes](https://characterdesignnotes.blogspot.com/2010/11/how-to-kill-great-character-design-part.html)). And set straight lines against curves, more strongly in stylized work than in realistic work ([Animator Island](https://www.animatorisland.com/perfect-posing-2-appealing-and-readable/)), because two parallel curves of equal radius give the "sausage" look.

Shape language (circles relaxed, angles tense) should still read in greyscale ([RocketBrush](https://rocketbrush.com/blog/shape-language-in-game-character-design-how-to-make-characters-readable-and-consistent)). The same blog's "70/30 silhouette rule" has no study behind it and should not be quoted.

### Proportions and appeal

| Style | Heads tall | Use it when | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toy chibi | 2 | Maximum cute, no prop to read | [Clip Studio](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/4973) |
| Standard chibi | 2.5 | Default kawaii figurine or charm | [Clip Studio](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/4868) |
| Chibi-lite | 3 | Pose or prop must read, e.g. Cupid with bow and wings | [Clip Studio](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/4973) |
| Real infant / putto basis | ~4 | Semi-realistic cherub | [DrawingHowToDraw](https://www.drawinghowtodraw.com/drawing-lessons/drawing-faces-lessons/articles/proportions-children-figures.html) |

The case for a 2.5–3-head Cupid follows from Clip Studio's note that 3 heads suits a character whose pose or costume must read. A 2-head body leaves too little room for bow, wings and a bent-leg pose in one outline.

Eyes sit at the middle of the head or lower, oversized and not far apart. The neck is omitted or short, the nose a dot at most, and the hands are mittens with fingers dropped ([Clip Studio general guide](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/4863)). These conventions match the strongest evidence in the research. Glocker et al. (2009) manipulated infant photographs and found that faces with more "baby schema" were rated cuter and drew stronger caretaking motivation. The features were a round face, high forehead, big eyes, small nose and small mouth ([Glocker et al. 2009](https://www.cceb.upenn.edu/csa/assets/user-content/documents/BabySchemainInfantFacesInducesCutenessPerceptionandMotivationforCaretakinginAdults.pdf)). They also suit FDM: short, chunky limbs are both cuter and harder to snap, which is what `tools/fragility.py` checks. Give the masses clearly different sizes (huge head, medium body, small limbs), following Bancroft's emphasis on shape and size variance ([Class Central: Bancroft](https://www.classcentral.com/course/skillshare-character-design-with-personality-4-core-concepts-for-creating-better-characters-with-tom-bancroft-81726)).

### Silhouettes for cut-outs and pendants

Fill the character solid black. If the pose is unclear as a black shape, the design is not finished ([Binus DKV](https://binus.ac.id/bandung/dkv/2025/11/04/the-power-of-silhouette-designing-readable-characters-in-motion/)). Gaps between limbs and torso keep the shape from becoming a blob ([RocketBrush](https://rocketbrush.com/blog/shape-language-in-game-character-design-how-to-make-characters-readable-and-consistent)). For a flying Cupid that means a near-profile or shallow 3/4 view, both wings fanned up and back with visible air between each trailing edge and the body, one leg tucked and one trailing with a hole between them, and an arrow that crosses no edge at a tangent. A frontal, symmetric pose with arms against the torso fails every one of these. Laser-cutting vendors size the ties between cut-outs at 0.5–1× material thickness ([SendCutSend](https://sendcutsend.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/5.2.pdf); [Ponoko](https://help.ponoko.com/en/articles/3204835-how-do-i-design-for-laser-cut-metal)). An FDM charm should use the shop's 1.2 mm handled-feature floor instead. Prefer two or three large, meaningful cut-throughs (under the wing, inside the bow) to many small ones.

### Clean Bézier outlines in code

Type designers' path rules give the most precise guidance found ([Glyphs: Drawing Good Paths](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths)). Put anchors at the horizontal and vertical extremes and make handles collinear at smooth points. Keep every segment inside the triangle formed by its two anchors and the point where its handles' extensions meet. Keep the two handles on one segment balanced, at about **55–56% of the distance to that intersection for circular arcs** and up to 60–70% for fuller shapes, and avoid inflections inside a segment. The 55% figure is the standard quarter-circle constant, κ ≈ 0.5523 ([Grida](https://grida.co/docs/math/kappa)); Mortensen's 0.551915 cuts the maximum radial error about fivefold ([Mortensen](https://spencermortensen.com/articles/bezier-circle/)). Either error is invisible at pendant scale. Put an S-curve's inflection on an anchor between two C segments, and use true corners only where the design wants a point (wing tip, arrow, heart). Lumps come from handles that are too long, flat spots from handles of unequal length, and kinks from smooth-point handles that are not collinear, so each segment can be checked programmatically against the triangle rule. When sampling the curves into OpenSCAD polygons, sample by arc length or curvature rather than uniform t, which faceting has already punished in this repo's 3d-print-design skill.

## At 60 mm, a realistic face is three 2 mm bands, so model planes

Real adults are about 7–7.5 heads tall, idealized figures 8, heroic and fashion figures 8.5–9 ([ZBrushCentral](https://www.zbrushcentral.com/t/human-anatomy-proportions-basic-guide/255099); [Proko](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/human-proportions-idealistic-figures/notes)). Children run from about 4 heads at age 1 to about 6 at age 5 and 7 at age 12 ([USF ClipArt ETC, Dunlop 1912](https://etc.usf.edu/clipart/52100/52139/52139_head.htm); [Drafting manual](https://draftingmanuals.tpub.com/14262/css/Measuring-The-Child-Figure-With-The-Head-149.htm)). No published canon for Renaissance putti was found, so about 4 heads, the infant figure, is a reasonable inference. In the Loomis 8-head canon, nipples, navel and crotch each sit one head below the one above. The shoulders are about 2⅓ heads wide and the hips 1.5 ([Proko](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/human-proportions-idealistic-figures/notes)). The wrist lines up with the crotch, the hand is about as long as the face from chin to hairline, and the foot is about forearm length ([scienceinsights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-proportion-a-body-for-figure-drawing/)).

| Figure | Heads tall | Head height at 60 mm | At 120 mm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Infant / putto-like | ~4 | 15 mm | 30 mm |
| 5–7-year-old | ~6 | 10 mm | 20 mm |
| Real adult | 7–7.5 | 8.0–8.6 mm | 16–17 mm |
| Idealized adult | 8 | 7.5 mm | 15 mm |
| Heroic / fashion | 8.5–9 | 6.7–7.1 mm | 13–14 mm |

*(Millimetre columns are arithmetic on the cited ratios.)*

The head follows Loomis: three equal facial thirds (hairline to brow, brow to nose base, nose base to chin), ears between brow and nose base, and about five eye-widths across with one eye-width between the eyes ([Proko: head front view](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/how-to-draw-the-head-front-view/notes)). The planes model adds the parts that survive at small scale: the brow ridge, eye sockets angling up to the "keystone", the cheekbone as a long C-curve from ear to jaw, and a "denture sphere" with the chin mass below it ([Proko: Planes and Forms of the Head](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/planes-and-forms-of-the-head/)).

The arithmetic decides the method. A 60 mm, 8-head figure has a 7.5 mm head, so each facial third is about 2 mm, roughly five extrusion widths. Lips, nostrils and eyelids cannot be resolved there. What prints is the brow shelf, the eye-socket shadow, the nose wedge and the chin. The shop's rule that domes under 2 mm tall print as stairs at 0.20 mm layers, and that faces and hands want 0.12 or 0.08 mm layers, applies directly (`DETAIL_STANDARD.md` rule 4).

Miniature sculptors solved this problem long ago by cheating. "Heroic scale" enlarges heads, thickens hands and feet, broadens shoulders and chunks up weapons so the figure reads "at arm's length" ([TabletopXtra](https://tabletopxtra.co.uk/blogs/news/28mm-vs-32mm-vs-35mm-a-heroic-scale-buyers-guide-for-3d-printed-miniatures)). Tamiya 1:48 and Warlord 1:56 figures differ markedly in height, yet their heads are "almost interchangeable" ([Warlord forum](https://www.warlordgames.com/community/topic/stop-the-madness-heroically-over-sized-miniatures/page/2/)). That implies roughly a 15% head oversize, which works out to a heroic build nearer 6–7 heads than 8 (this report's arithmetic, not a published canon). Sculptors also hollow the eye sockets before setting the eye, and cut recesses deeper than true anatomy so the shadows land where the eye expects them ([Proko: sculpting the eye](https://proko.com/course-lesson/what-to-look-for-when-sculpting-the-eye)). FDM designers push scale further still. One designs minis at 40 mm "for clean results on a 0.4 mm nozzle" ([MakerWorld 3043736](https://makerworld.com/en/models/3043736)). Hands are where small figures usually go wrong: at 60 mm the hand is about 5.6 mm long and each finger well under 1 mm thick, so below about 100 mm a fused mitten with a separate thumb is the honest choice.

**Wings** need real anatomy if they are to look like wings. A wing is an arm. About ten long, pointed primaries attach to the hand bones and rounder secondaries to the forearm; no flight feathers attach to the upper arm. The coverts lie in overlapping rows over the bases, and the alula sits on the thumb ([Clip Studio: Feathered Wings](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/7950)). The reading cue at thumbnail size is the forward-pointing wrist corner on the leading edge plus the long primary fan. In print, model the feathers as overlapping slabs, each stepped proud of the next in the documented overlap direction, never as separated blades. **Quadrupeds** are two masses, ribcage and a forward-tilted pelvis, on a flexible spine. The ribcage is about half the torso length and usually drawn too small, and the legs must zig-zag or the animal reads as a table ([Drawabox, via search summary](https://www.drawabox.com/lesson/5/2)).

## Shop-measured limits beat web rules that disagree by 2×

The published FDM numbers are rules of thumb that conflict. Forge Labs asks for 1 mm-wide embossing ([Forge Labs](https://forgelabs.com/design-guides/fdm)), Layer X 0.5 mm ([Layer X](https://layerx3d.in/blog/fdm-design-rules-wall-thickness-overhangs-bridging-tolerances)), and text minimums run from 4 to 10 mm ([Mandarin3D](https://mandarin3d.com/blog/text-and-engravings-best-practices-for-readable-3d-printed-text); [Prusa forum](https://forum.prusa3d.com/forum/original-prusa-i3-mk3s-mk3-how-do-i-print-this-printing-help/printing-small-text/)). The shop's own coupon gives tighter numbers, specific to the P1S profile. It was sliced at three layer heights and its toolpaths measured. The coupon has not yet been printed and photographed, so these are toolpath results, not plastic. Use them.

| Feature | Published range | Shop rule (`DETAIL_STANDARD.md`) |
|---|---|---|
| Lettering height | 4–10 mm | **3 mm**; 2 mm only on an up-facing face; 1.5 mm on a 0.2 mm nozzle (rule 10) |
| Separate dot, pupil, colour island | ~5 mm for AMS colour detail ([Geeky Inc](https://www.geekyinc.com/multi-color-3d-printing-anime-figures-the-ams-guide-for-p1s-x1c-2026/)) | **≥ 0.8 mm**; 0.4–0.6 mm dots are dropped (rule 11); ~1.5 mm preferred for pupils |
| Groove width | ≥ 0.5 mm | **≥ 0.4 mm** keeps full depth (0.5 for margin) (rule 5) |
| Handled or proud feature | ≥ 0.8–1 mm walls ([Hubs](https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/key-design-considerations-3d-printing/)) | **≥ 1.2 mm** (3 beads); isolated pin ≥ 1.0 mm (rule 5) |
| Level heights | not stated | **Multiples of 0.2 mm**; a 0.3 mm groove printed 0.4 (rule 2) |
| Visible relief depth | 0.3–0.5 mm | **0.4–0.6 mm joints, 0.6 mm courses, 0.8–1.2 mm frames** (rule 6) |
| Overhang | ~45° ([Hubs](https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-parts-fdm-3d-printing/)) | **Design to ~37°**; exactly 45° drew 29,332 support moves (rule 7) |
| Round features | — | True curves only where ≥ 2 mm tall; smaller ones faceted or chamfered (rule 4) |

Two findings change how to spend effort. First, **the nozzle sets the smallest shape and the layer height the smallest step**: dropping from 0.20 to 0.08 mm layers on the 0.4 nozzle did almost nothing for letters or dots, while a 0.2 mm nozzle halved the minimums (rule 12; `FINE_DETAIL_RESEARCH.md`). Second, **XY detail is precise and Z detail is quantized**. A groove running up a vertical face held its depth to ±0.01 mm at every layer height, while level edges snapped to whole layers. The shop's three detail sizes line up with the character-art hierarchy: *form* over 10 mm is the primary mass, *feature* 2–10 mm is the secondary form, and *texture* 0.4–2 mm is the tertiary detail (rule 8).

**Translating 2D art into relief** follows the digital bas-relief literature. Weyrich et al. (SIGGRAPH 2007) compress depth *gradients* nonlinearly rather than depth values, which keeps outlines and fine detail in a very shallow height field ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Digital-bas-relief-from-3D-scenes-Weyrich-Deng/67aa2cf15f88989047276c71f5c1247d4e28d303)). Related methods find the silhouette first and shrink large depth gaps so the relief has no cliffs ([Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/58823/1/basrelief_11_16.pdf)). For FDM, this means putting the readable information in the outline and in the edge slopes, where the nozzle places walls accurately, and keeping it out of subtle height changes across the field, which become 0.2 mm terraces. Coin relief of about 0.3–0.5 mm ([shopglobalcoin](https://shopglobalcoin.com/blogs/blog/beyond-relief-pushing-the-boundaries-of-coin-design)) is only two layers deep. A figurative relief needs a few millimetres for a domed belly or cheek to read as a curve. Separate the planes with a step of a whole number of layers at the outline, then compress the interior. Classical undercuts are overhangs on FDM, so replace them with a chamfered or stepped edge that throws a similar shadow line.

**Orientation decides which detail survives.** Printed face-up, the outline is sharp and every height is a layer step, which is best for charms, raised outlines and lettering. Printed upright, the relief profile is placed accurately and the outline becomes stepped, which is best for portrait plaques; the shop's letters scored 0.92 face-up against 0.86 upright. A working charm spec, synthesised from published guides and the shop rules, is 3–5 mm thick and printed flat ([Vextrude](https://vextrude.com/blog-3d-printed-keychain)), with a 4–5 mm ring hole surrounded by about 3 mm of material ([JLC3DP](https://jlc3dp.com/blog/3d-printed-keychains-tips)) and a chamfer of a few layers on the bed edge rather than a fillet ([Hydra Research](https://www.hydraresearch3d.com/design-rules)). Raised detail stands 0.6–1.0 mm proud and at least 1.2 mm wide; engraved lines are at least 0.5 mm wide and 0.4–0.6 mm deep.

Ironing smooths only flat tops, so it suits a charm's field and not a domed relief. Vendors claim matte PLA hides layer lines better than silk, but that has not been tested in a controlled way ([Siddament](https://siddament.com.au/blogs/filament-guide-au/matte-vs-silk-pla)).

## Closing plus `smooth_path` for charms, smooth-min SDFs for figurines

The deciding fact is that **CSG cannot blend 3D solids smoothly**. `union()` always leaves a crease, `hull()` and `minkowski()` round only convex shapes, and nothing in OpenSCAD 2021's CSG fillets a concave join. In 2D the picture is much better. A local test on a cat-head silhouette (head circle plus ears and chin, `$fs=0.2`) measured three outlines:

| Outline | Max turn at one vertex | Max curvature jump | Fidelity to raw union |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw `union()` | 80.2° | — (6 crease vertices > 20°) | reference |
| Closing `offset(r=-3) offset(r=3)` | 3.8° | 0.73 /mm (G1) | IoU 0.997, Hausdorff 0.59 mm |
| BOSL2 `smooth_path(..., method="corners")`, 10 points | 5.1° | **0.15 /mm (G2)** | IoU 0.831, Hausdorff 7.96 mm |

The closing removes creases while leaving the shape almost untouched. The spline gives curvature-continuous highlights, about five times smoother at the joins, but it drifts unless its control points come from the blocked-out shape. So the charm recipe is to block out with circles and ellipses, apply a closing (Rc 1.5–3 mm) to fillet the creases and an opening (at least one nozzle width) to soften sharp convex points, then retrace hero outlines in G2 with `smooth_path`. Gate that retrace at IoU ≥ 0.97 and Hausdorff ≤ 0.5 mm against the approved outline (thresholds proposed, not sourced). Finally, extrude with BOSL2 `offset_sweep(..., top=os_smooth(...))` for a pillowy domed edge.

BOSL2's "smooth" rounding uses 4th-order Béziers for continuous curvature, and the library is BSD-2-Clause, safe for commercial output ([BOSL2](https://github.com/BelfrySCAD/BOSL2); [rounding.scad](https://github.com/BelfrySCAD/BOSL2/blob/master/rounding.scad)).

For **chubby 3D figurines**, build a signed-distance model in Python from spheres, capsules and tapered round-cones, blended with a polynomial smooth-min whose `k` is the fillet width in mm. The quadratic form, `h = clamp(0.5 + 0.5*(a-b)/k, 0, 1); mix(a, b, h) - k*h*(1-h)`, blends only where the two distances differ by less than k ([vgracianos](https://vgracianos.github.io/blog/smin)). At a == b it sits k/4 below the hard minimum, which is what forms the fillet web and slightly fattens the join ([Stranathan](https://ianstranathan.github.io/html/Graphics/polynomial-smin)). That fattening is one more reason to measure a smoothed body against its reference. fogleman/sdf (MIT) packages these primitives with `union(a, b, k=...)` and marching-cubes meshing ([fogleman/sdf](https://github.com/fogleman/sdf)). In the local tests, a chibi test body meshed with scikit-image marching cubes converged in volume by a **0.25 mm voxel size in 1.6 s**. It was not watertight until grid samples lying exactly on the zero level were nudged (`D[abs(D)<1e-9] = 1e-6`), because grid-exact zeros, here from the flat base cut, produce degenerate triangles. `manifold3d.Manifold.level_set`, already installed, always produced a watertight mesh but ran about 300× slower through a per-point Python callback, and it treats positive values as inside.

Suggested blend widths, which are inference: k of 1.5–5 mm at necks and limbs and 0.8–1.5 mm at ears. Below about 0.6 mm a fillet probably will not print as a visible curve. Carve eyes and mouth as smooth subtractions at least 0.8 mm deep. Do not copy Inigo Quilez's "Happy Jumping" shader code. Its header forbids commercial use, and its own comment admits the character goes "off-model very often" ([shader port header](https://git.rustybever.be/vieter-v/v/raw/commit/04c4f16f75b7a6eb20fcd888d05eeec50cc0dcae/examples/sokol/04_multi_shader_glsl/rt_glsl_puppy.glsl)). Reimplementing the standard math is fine.

**Blender headless** is the second-best route, and better when the pose is easiest to set from a stick figure. A Skin modifier, Subsurf 2, Voxel Remesh at 0.4 mm and Smooth ×5 gave a watertight single body in 0.6 s locally. But a skin radius of 9 came out at only ±7.5 × ±5.6 mm, so the shrink is uneven between axes and must be calibrated ([Blender API: MeshSkinVertex](https://docs.blender.org/api/blender_python_api_2_74_5/bpy.types.MeshSkinVertex.html)). Metaballs are worse. Element radius is a field radius, not a surface radius, and in the local test two requested 5–6 mm lobes came out about 3.4 mm and split into two bodies. The practical split is to do the organic core as an SDF or skin mesh, then `import()` it into OpenSCAD only for the mechanical parts: flat base, keyring loop, maker's mark. Check the 3D result against the skill's existing smooth-organic target (mean dihedral under 4°, p95 under 9°) and trimesh's `is_watertight` and `body_count == 1`. **Semi-realistic figures are where code runs out.** Scripted bases plus noise displacement are the ceiling; real faces need hand sculpting.

## A twelve-step loop with a check at every stage

Kidrobot's production pipeline is the clearest primary account. The steps are rough turnarounds that settle raised versus engraved features, then a sculpt photographed from each angle, then written notes reviewed by the designer and artist. The yardstick is that **"each round, the number of comments … is half as many as the round before"** ([Kidrobot](https://blog.kidrobot.com/how-a-toy-gets-made/)). Kidrobot also warns that scale surprises teams who see a physical object only late. The table adapts this sequence to the shop's code tools and gates. The gates are the condition for moving on.

| Step | Action | Gate before moving on | Cupid failure it blocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One-sentence brief: character, single emotion, product type, true size | Size class fixed in mm | Scale surprise |
| 2 | Reference board of several sources, logged with licence (see legal section) | Provenance file started | Copying one source |
| 3 | 4–5 black-fill thumbnails of different forms, per the shop's approval rule; Scott picks one | Chosen by silhouette alone | Weak concept |
| 4 | Skeleton as named parameters: head count, eye line, joint angles, knee direction | No two joint angles equal or mirrored within ~5°; knee test passes | Twinning, backwards legs |
| 5 | Blockout of primary masses only (circles/ellipses or SDF primitives) | Black fill reads front, side and 3/4 | Clutter |
| 6 | Open negative space; resolve every near-touch | Every pair either overlaps decisively or is separated by a gap that reads at size; ties ≥ 1.2 mm | Bow touching face |
| 7 | Secondary forms; overlay the reference at 50% | Ratios measured, not judged by eye | Bulging back |
| 8 | Smooth the outline (closing/opening, then `smooth_path`) | IoU ≥ 0.97, Hausdorff ≤ 0.5 mm vs step-7 outline | Smoothing drift |
| 9 | Tertiary detail only above `DETAIL_STANDARD.md` | "Say it or don't" applied | Clutter |
| 10 | Extrude or relief; heights on the 0.2 mm grid | `product_gate.py`, `print_fidelity.py`, `fragility.py` clean or explained | Unprintable detail |
| 11 | Fixed review renders: front, side, 3/4, top, black fill, 150–200 px thumbnail, as-printed still | Same cameras every round | Unfair comparisons |
| 12 | Numbered notes, closed one by one | Notes roughly halve each round | Endless fiddling |

**Self-critique checks.** The fresh-eye tricks come from James Gurney: turn the work upside down (he spends about a quarter of his painting time working inverted), squint, use a reducing glass or a mirror, photograph it and convert it to two values, and ask someone what they notice first ([Gurney Journey](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2008/08/getting-fresh-eye.html)). In code each of these becomes a render: a mirrored render (flip the reference too, per [Painter's Keys](https://painterskeys.com/?p=6898)), a 2–3-value flat-lit render, a thumbnail at roughly Etsy search size, a 45° turntable, and a black fill from five angles. Polycount adds that a "smooth" silhouette reads as weak and should be pushed and tilted ([Polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/comment/2581167)). The dominant beginner failure is subdividing or detailing before the blockout is right, which produces lumpy forms. The fix is to drop back to the lowest level and make the big changes there ([Polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/comment/1878957/)). In OpenSCAD terms, go back to primitives and hulls before raising `$fn`. A few checks can be scripted: a mirror test (reflect the outline and compute IoU; above about 0.9 means twinned), a morphological opening by half the minimum feature width (anything that vanishes is too thin), and a lobe test (if deleting a part barely changes the union outline, the part is not reading). These thresholds are proposed and unvalidated. Nobody has measured how well any of these tests work. They are practitioner consensus.

**Handling Scott's circled feedback.** The CGMA guidance is to look past the requested fix to the underlying issue, ask when a note is unclear rather than nodding along, build what the client asked for alongside your own alternative, and acknowledge every note ([CGMA](https://cgmasteracademy.com/blog/how-to-read-minds-and-decode-artistic-feedback.html)). Isolate the problem area and redline it rather than redoing the whole piece ([Blender Durian paint-over revision](https://durian.blender.org/concept-art/paint-over-revision)). Track each round so earlier notes are visibly closed ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/2d-game-art-work-feedbacking-and-management-tool)). Applied to a circled render, each circle becomes one numbered line that describes the problem, not the solution: "3. Back: bulges past the wing root." Name the principle (proportion, tangent, hinge, clutter, smoothing drift). Measure it before touching it, for example the back's offset from the hip–shoulder line in millimetres against the reference. Change only that region. Then show before and after from the identical camera, with silhouette and thumbnail versions when the note was about readability. The Cupid's worsening back is exactly what this protocol stops: a fix that has to beat a recorded number cannot quietly make the bulge larger. If round N+1 has as many notes as round N, the form is wrong, and the work goes back to step 5, not to more polishing.

## Copyright protects this Cupid's drawing, never the idea of Cupid

The US Copyright Office's Compendium protects a character's "original, visual aspects", such as "facial features and specific body shape", clothing and other visual elements. It does not protect the name, the general idea, or "character concept, idea, or style" (§911). It lists "sculptures based on drawings" as derivative works (§907.1). Jewelry is protected for its creative shape and colour but not for a "mere variation on a common or standardized design or familiar symbol" (§908) ([Compendium ch. 900](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap900/ch900-visual-art.pdf)). Courts have treated an artistic style as an unprotectable idea ([Mondaq on Jangle Vision v. Alexander Wang](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/copyright/1398678/two-courts-two-ad-campaigns-and-copyrights-idea-expression-dichotomy)). So a chubby winged boy with a bow is free to draw. Someone's specific drawn Cupid is not. Turning that drawing into a relief adds authorship but does not launder the copying. The idea/expression line is hard to draw in practice, and one UK case found infringement without direct copying of expression ([IR Global](https://irglobal.com/article/understanding-the-idea-expression-dichotomy-in-copyright-law/)). None of this is legal advice.

Safe practice follows from that. Study several references to learn the subject, such as how a wing joins the shoulder, then build a new form; never trace or height-map a single copyrighted image. When a close adaptation is wanted, start from public-domain sources: the Met's Open Access CC0 images may be modified and distributed "for any purpose, including commercial" ([The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/policies/image-resources)), while Smithsonian CC0 items carry a liability disclaimer, so check each item's rights statement ([My Modern Met](https://mymodernmet.com/smithsonian-open-access-public-domain/)). Keep a provenance file per design (references viewed, URLs, licences, the shop's own sketches or prompts), which closes the provenance-manifest gap CLAUDE.md already flags. Avoid named or licensed characters entirely. The One Piece and FNAF keychains on Printables were cited in the research for technique only, and they show exactly the takedown risk: on a report, Etsy removes the listing first without judging the claim, and repeat reports can cost account privileges ([Etsy Help](https://help.etsy.com/hc/en-us/articles/360034079714-What-to-Do-if-You-Receive-a-Notice-of-Intellectual-Property-Infringement)).

**Etsy's rule is stricter than copyright, and its date in CLAUDE.md conflicts with the evidence.** The Creativity Standards require items made with computerized tools, which includes 3D printers, to be "produced based on a seller's original design". Reselling purchased files or bundles does not count, even with a commercial licence ([Listadum summary](https://www.listadum.com/blog/etsy-creativity-standards); primary page [etsy.com/legal/creativity](https://www.etsy.com/legal/creativity), which returned 403 and was not read directly). CLAUDE.md attributes this to an "Aug 11, 2026 Prohibited Items Policy update". Growtsy, however, quotes the Creativity Standards as "Last updated on Jun 10, 2025". By its account, the 11 August 2026 Prohibited Items change concerned Section 2, animal products (a natural-fur ban), and said nothing about computerized tools ([Growtsy](https://www.growtsy.com/etsy-changes)). Blogs such as ShieldMyShop claim an August 2026 original-design rule ([ShieldMyShop](https://www.shieldmyshop.com/blog/2026-06-30-etsy-original-design-rule-cricut-laser-cutter-3d-printer-sellers-august-2026)). Growtsy's reading is better documented, but this report treats the date as **unresolved until someone reads Etsy's primary page**. The requirement is in force either way. Two corollaries: a CC0 image used as-is may still fail Etsy's "seller's original design" test, so substantially rework public-domain bases; and the research did not establish whether an AI-generated design counts as "original" for this rule.

## Conclusion

The Cupid rounds did not fail for lack of skill. They failed because judgment ran where measurement should have. Each defect had a numeric test that could have caught it before Scott did: a joint-angle and knee-side check for the legs, a near-touch check for the bow, an IoU/Hausdorff comparison against the approved outline for the back, and the say-it-or-don't and real-size tests for the clutter. This shop already runs gates for printability (`product_gate`, `print_fidelity`, `fragility`). What it lacks is an equivalent layer for drawing quality, and most of it can be scripted with shapely and trimesh, which are already installed. The one consistent message across animation, toy and miniature practice is that small, printed characters reward exaggeration and simplification: bigger heads, chunkier limbs, deeper sockets, fewer and larger shapes. That is also what an FDM nozzle prints best. Appeal and printability point the same way.

Two cautions shape the next step. The shop's detail limits are toolpath measurements, not photographs of printed plastic, so printing and photographing the calibration coupon would turn the strongest numbers in this report into verified ones. And the silhouette heuristics proposed here (mirror IoU above 0.9, a 5° joint-angle rule, the 0.97 IoU smoothing gate) are reasonable starting points with no published validation. Calibrate them against the Cupid drafts Scott has already circled. If they flag those drafts and pass the fixed one, they are worth keeping.
