# Realistic Human and Animal Figure Fundamentals for Small Printed Figurines (30-150 mm)

Research notes, 2026-11-03. Sources are figure-drawing teaching sites (Proko, Clip Studio Tips), a 1912 anatomy text reproduced by USF's ClipArt ETC, miniature-hobby retailer guides and forums. Many search results were low-quality content-farm pages (peoplespharmacy, fightcancer.org, internationaltrucks, simplon, sothebysinstitute mirrors). I excluded them and did not cite them. Primary books (Loomis, Bridgman, Hampton, Zarins, Reilly) were **not** read directly in this session. Their content is reached only through Proko and tutorial summaries, so treat book attributions as secondhand.

Numbers in the "Inferences" sections that convert ratios into millimetres for print scale are my arithmetic on the cited ratios, not sourced figures.

## 1. Figure proportion systems (heads-tall canons) and body landmarks

### Takeaway
Real adults are about 7-7.5 heads tall. Idealized figures are drawn at 8, and heroic/fashion figures at 8.5-9. Children run from about 4 heads at birth or age 1, to about 6 heads at age 5-7, to about 7 heads at age 12. In the 8-head canon each torso landmark sits one head apart: chin, then nipples, then navel, then crotch. The crotch is at or just below mid-height. The wrist lines up with the crotch, and the elbow with the waist or bottom of the ribcage.

### Cited Findings
- Idealized figures are usually drawn "8 or more heads tall rather than 7.5." — [Proko, Human Proportions: Idealistic Figures (notes)](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/human-proportions-idealistic-figures/notes)
- Loomis torso landmarks, which Proko describes as similar to Richer's: nipples one head below the chin, navel a second head below that, crotch a third head below. — [Proko notes](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/human-proportions-idealistic-figures/notes)
- Loomis widths: shoulders "2⅓ heads" wide, a bit wider than Richer's; hips 1.5 heads wide. — [Proko notes](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/human-proportions-idealistic-figures/notes)
- A commenter on the Proko page reports that a female figure drawn at 7 heads looked "stumpy," and that it should be 8 heads like the male. They attribute the shorter real average to a smaller skull, so the same head count still works. This is a commenter's opinion, not Proko's instruction. — [Proko notes](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/human-proportions-idealistic-figures/notes)
- Real people vary from about 7 to 7.5 heads tall. Heroic and fashion figures are stretched to 8.5 or even 9 heads. — [ZBrushCentral, Human anatomy proportions basic guide](https://www.zbrushcentral.com/t/human-anatomy-proportions-basic-guide/255099); [Fiveable, essential human figure proportions](https://fiveable.me/lists/essential-human-figure-proportions)
- The midpoint of the body falls at the pubic bone in the male and slightly above the pubic bone in the female. A search summary of the same page says the midpoint "lines up with the top of the pubic hair." — [pxstudio, Comparative Proportions in the Male and Female Figure](https://pxstudio.us/blog/?p=16404)
- The nipple line varies considerably in women depending on breast size. — [pxstudio](https://pxstudio.us/blog/?p=16404)
- In one guide the knees fall near the sixth head length, and the elbows line up roughly with the navel. Sources vary on this. — [Fiveable study guide](https://fiveable.me/drawing-foundations/unit-10/proportions-human-body/study-guide/bVYcw96jRRpCUZJk)
- Elbow placement: other sources put the elbow at the waist or bottom of the ribcage rather than the navel. Sources agree that the wrist aligns with the crotch / hip joint line. — [Fiveable study guide](https://fiveable.me/drawing-foundations/unit-10/proportions-human-body/study-guide/bVYcw96jRRpCUZJk); [scienceinsights, How to Proportion a Body](https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-proportion-a-body-for-figure-drawing/)
- Hand length, from the base of the palm to the tip of the middle finger, roughly equals face length from chin to hairline (not the full skull). — [scienceinsights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-proportion-a-body-for-figure-drawing/); [Wikipedia, Body proportions](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Body_proportion)
- Foot length is about forearm length, from the inside elbow crease to the wrist. Other sources say the foot is about one head long instead, so this rule is not universal. — [scienceinsights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-proportion-a-body-for-figure-drawing/)
- Children, from Dunlop's *Anatomical Diagrams for the Use of Art Students* (1912): head about 1/4 of height at birth, about 1/6 at 5 years, about 1/7½ in the adult. — [USF ClipArt ETC, Relation of Head to Height by Age](https://etc.usf.edu/clipart/52100/52139/52139_head.htm)
- A one-year-old is about four heads tall. — [Drafting manual, Measuring the Child Figure with the Head](https://draftingmanuals.tpub.com/14262/css/Measuring-The-Child-Figure-With-The-Head-149.htm)
- At 12 years the figure is about 7 head units high. — [Drafting manual](https://draftingmanuals.tpub.com/14262/css/Measuring-The-Child-Figure-With-The-Head-149.htm)
- An artist-training handout lists a toddler at about 5 heads (age not specified), about 5.5 heads at an intermediate stage, and 6 heads for ages 8-12. This conflicts slightly with the "6 heads at age 5" figure above. — [RTB Rwanda, Apply Anatomy for Plastician Artist (PDF)](https://www.elearning.rtb.gov.rw/pluginfile.php/6709/mod_resource/content/1/ARCCSC%205001-CSCAD501%20%20APPLY%20ANATOMY%20FOR%20PLASTOCIAN%20ARTIST%20%20L5SCE.pdf)
- Putto: a naked, sometimes winged, cherubic infant boy. The Renaissance form was revived from the classical Eros/Cupid, with Donatello credited with reintroducing it. Cherubs were often shown as winged heads alone. — [Wikipedia, Putto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putto); [Merriam-Webster, Putti](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Putti)

### Inferences
- **Quick table for designers** (synthesis of the cited ratios):

  | Figure | Heads tall | Head height in a 60 mm figure | Head height in a 120 mm figure |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Newborn / 1 yr / putto-like infant | ~4 | 15 mm | 30 mm |
  | Toddler (~3 yr) | ~5 (uncertain) | 12 mm | 24 mm |
  | 5-7 yr child | ~6 | 10 mm | 20 mm |
  | 12 yr | ~7 | 8.6 mm | 17 mm |
  | Real adult | 7-7.5 | 8.0-8.6 mm | 16-17 mm |
  | Idealized adult | 8 | 7.5 mm | 15 mm |
  | Heroic / fashion | 8.5-9 | 6.7-7.1 mm | 13-14 mm |

- At figurine scale, the long canons (8.5-9 heads) make the head and face smallest exactly where print resolution is weakest. For a 30-60 mm print, a 7-7.5 head realistic canon, or a shorter heroic-miniature build (section 3), will keep the face legible better than a "heroic" 9-head fashion canon.
- **Landmark checklist for an 8-head adult**, measured from the top of the head in head units: chin 1, nipples 2, navel 3, crotch / pubic bone 4 (mid-height), wrist level with crotch, elbow at waist / bottom of ribcage (about 3), knee about 6, soles 8. Shoulders about 2⅓ heads wide (male, Loomis), hips about 1.5 heads.
- Hand ≈ face (chin to hairline) is the most useful quick check on a sculpt. Hands in a small figure are almost always made too small by eye.
- No proportion number for putti was found. Since putti represent infants, the age 1-2 range (about 4 heads) is a reasonable starting point. That is inference, not a sourced canon.

### Gaps
- No primary-source reading of Loomis's *Figure Drawing for All It's Worth*, Bridgman, or Hampton. Knee height, pit-of-neck position, and male/female differences beyond the midpoint were not confirmed from a primary source.
- No sourced age-3 head count (only an unlabeled "toddler ≈ 5").
- No sourced head-count canon specifically for Renaissance putti or cherubs. Museum and art-historical catalogues would be needed.

## 2. Head construction (Loomis, Reilly, Asaro planes) and feature placement

### Takeaway
The Loomis construction uses a cranial ball with flattened side planes. The face divides into three equal vertical sections: hairline to brow, brow to nose base, nose base to chin. The ears sit in the middle third, between brow and nose base. Horizontally the head is about five eye-widths wide, with one eye-width between the eyes. A planes model (Asaro-style) breaks the head into the brow ridge, eye sockets angling up toward the "keystone," a cheekbone C-curve from the top of the ear to the front of the jaw, nose planes, and a "denture sphere" with the chin mass below it.

### Cited Findings
- Loomis side plane: its height should be "⅔ of the circle." Half the side plane equals ⅓ of the face. Measure that distance down from the brow line to find the nose base, again to find the chin, and the same distance up from the brow to find the hairline. — [Proko, How to Draw the Head – Front View (notes)](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/how-to-draw-the-head-front-view/notes)
- Ears sit in the middle third and usually end at the outside of the original circle. In a down-tilt they move into the bottom half of the oval. The ear-versus-brow relationship shows head tilt: ears level with the brow means the brow is at mid-circle, and ears below the brow means the head is tilted up. — [Proko front view notes](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/how-to-draw-the-head-front-view/notes)
- "The Loomis head is approximately 5 eyes wide, with 1 eye width between each eye." Further rules: the pupils track down to the mouth corners, and the inner eye corners (halfway between pupil and centre) track down to the corners of the nose. This comes from a Proko-staff reply in comments, not the lesson body. — [Proko front view notes](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/how-to-draw-the-head-front-view/notes)
- The face is in three roughly equal sections: hairline to brow, brow to nose base, nose base to chin. The mouth sits between nose and chin, "a little closer to the nose." — [Fine Art Tutorials, Loomis Method](https://finearttutorials.com/guide/loomis-method/); [Medium, How to Draw Heads with the Loomis Method](https://medium.com/@ramstudioscomics/how-to-draw-heads-with-the-andrew-loomis-method-78f9d04ab132)
- Eye-line conflict: some tutorials put the eyes at the halfway point of the head (crown to chin). Loomis-focused tutorials put them about a third of the way down from brow to nose base. These are not contradictory, because the brow-to-nose third lies just above mid-head, but tutorials state them inconsistently. — [Fine Art Tutorials, Loomis Method](https://finearttutorials.com/guide/loomis-method/); [Substack, Complete Guide to Loomis Method](https://bhaswanthkota.substack.com/p/the-complete-guide-to-loomis-method)
- Planes and forms of the head (Proko):
  - The brow line marks the transition from the front of the skull to the top. The temple is "most often visible as the pointy end of the brow."
  - The back of the head sits "one ear width behind the ear."
  - The ear is built with "5 lines into a D shape or C shape."
  - The TMJ and ear hole together look "like a little number 8."
  - The cheek is a "long c-curve" from the top of the ear ending at the front of the mandible, and the cheekbone starts as a triangle placeholder.
  - The eye sockets angle "up from the outside to the keystone"; extending those lines up through the brow ridge gives the forehead planes.
  - The nose has its own planes over the nasal aperture.
  - A "denture sphere" orients the face, with the chin mass placed below it.

  — [Proko, Planes and Forms of the Head](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/planes-and-forms-of-the-head/)
- Older anatomy text on the profile: the zygoma (cheekbone ridge) runs back from the cheekbone toward the ear, and a lesser ridge rises between the temple and the eye socket. — [Chest of Books, The Head: Planes / Profile](https://chestofbooks.com/arts/drawing/Anatomy/The-Head-Planes-The-Head-Profile-Planes-The-Eye-Comparisons.html)
- The Asaro head is credited to John Asaro, sculptor/anatomist, as a simplified planar model of the head. The pages making this attribution were low quality; it is widely repeated but I did not verify it from Asaro's own materials. — [Proko, Planes and Forms of the Head](https://www.proko.com/lesson/planes-and-forms-of-the-head) (no Asaro detail on the page itself)

### Inferences
- **Feature map for a sculpted head** (front view, head height H from crown to chin, using cited ratios):
  - The face (hairline to chin) is three equal thirds.
  - The eyes sit just below the brow line, near mid-head.
  - The nose base is two-thirds of the face down from the hairline.
  - The mouth slit is about one-third of the way from nose base to chin. "Closer to the nose" supports roughly this.
  - The ears span from brow to nose base, set behind the jaw-hinge midline.
  - Width is about 5 eye-widths. The mouth corners align under the pupils, and the nose wings align under the inner eye corners.
- **Profile silhouette checklist**, assembled from the planes findings: the forehead slopes back from the brow ridge; the brow ridge projects over a recessed eye socket; the nose projects furthest; the muzzle ("denture sphere") curves around the teeth; and the chin projects forward again under the lower lip. Getting the step-back at the eye socket and the step-back under the lower lip right is what makes a profile read as human at small scale. This ordering is my synthesis, since none of the fetched sources described profile construction step by step.
- At 7.5 mm head height (60 mm, 8-head figure), each facial third is about 2 mm. That is about five 0.4 mm extrusion widths, so on an FDM print, individual features (lips, nostrils, eyelids) cannot be resolved. Only the big planes survive: brow shelf, eye-socket shadow, nose wedge, chin. This argues for the planes approach over feature detail at this scale.

### Gaps
- Reilly abstraction rhythms were not found in any fetched source.
- No authoritative published Asaro landmark list was found. The Proko planes lesson does not cover profile construction in its notes (the full transcript wasn't available).
- No sourced numeric profile angles (e.g. facial angle, nose projection depth).

## 3. How miniature/figurine sculptors simplify and exaggerate anatomy

### Takeaway
"Heroic scale" miniatures deliberately exaggerate heads, hands, feet, shoulders and weapons so the figure reads at arm's length and is easy to paint. Sculptors hollow and deepen eye sockets and recesses so shadows land where the eye expects. "28 mm" is usually measured from sole to **eye level**, not to the top of the head. There are no governing standards, and I found no reliable published head-count canon for heroic miniatures.

### Cited Findings
- Scale is usually the height from the sole of the foot to the eye line of a standing human-sized figure, so a figure 32 mm to the top of its head may still be sold as 28 mm. 28 mm ≈ 1:56-1:64. There is no governing body, and the same nominal size varies between ranges. — [TabletopXtra, 28mm vs 32mm vs 35mm heroic scale buyers guide](https://tabletopxtra.co.uk/blogs/news/28mm-vs-32mm-vs-35mm-a-heroic-scale-buyers-guide-for-3d-printed-miniatures)
- Heroic scale exaggerates features so they "read clearly at arm's length across a tabletop": heads slightly larger than true anatomy, hands and feet thickened, shoulders broadened, weapons given chunkier barrels and blades. These sculpts are "far easier to paint and far more characterful" than anatomically correct figures of the same height. — [TabletopXtra](https://tabletopxtra.co.uk/blogs/news/28mm-vs-32mm-vs-35mm-a-heroic-scale-buyers-guide-for-3d-printed-miniatures)
- Anvil Industry's Regiments range: about 30.5 mm from base to top of head, equal to 28 mm to eye level. — [Anvil Industry, Regiments range guide](https://anvilindustry.co.uk/regiments-range-guide)
- Measured examples: Perry figure 28 mm, Warlord plastic 29 mm, Black Tree 31.5 mm. — [BLMA blog, Scale creep pt 3](https://www.blmablog.com/2009/03/scale-creep-pt-3-painting.html)
- Tamiya 1:48 figures and Warlord's 28 mm (1:56) figures differ significantly in height, yet "the heads are almost interchangeable." This is direct evidence that heroic miniatures oversize heads relative to body height. — [Warlord Games forum, Stop the madness: heroically over-sized miniatures](https://www.warlordgames.com/community/topic/stop-the-madness-heroically-over-sized-miniatures/page/2/)
- Forum comparison: 28 mm minis in general, and GW's in particular, have "rather overexaggerated heads and hands." A true 1:35-scale head is about the size of a GW Space Marine's head. — [Dakka Dakka forum thread](https://www.dakkadakka.com/dakkaforum/posts/list/713460.page)
- One sculptor's "32 mm" may look oversized and heroic while another's is closer to true scale. — [DM Minis, General Sizing & Scale Information](https://dmminisus.com/pages/sizing-information)
- From the painter's point of view, the face is the most important size. — search summary of [Dakka Dakka](https://www.dakkadakka.com/dakkaforum/posts/list/713460.page) / [CoolMiniOrNot, Sculpting in scale?](https://www.coolminiornot.com/forums/threads/sculpting-in-scale.7417/)
- Sculptors hollow out the eye sockets first, before setting the eye. Proko's sculpting lesson likewise shapes the socket before adding the eyeball. A skull-practice thread recommends deeper recesses around the sockets and bigger eyeballs. — [Proko, What to Look for When Sculpting the Eye](https://proko.com/course-lesson/what-to-look-for-when-sculpting-the-eye); [Polycount, WIP skull practice](https://polycount.com/discussion/148720/wip-skull-practice)
- "Many of the small details won't be captured at all at very small sizes." Intricate parts tend to end up oversized while sculpting small, so check fine features against a commercial model of the same scale. — [CoolMiniOrNot, Sculpting in scale?](https://www.coolminiornot.com/forums/threads/sculpting-in-scale.7417/)
- The most believable small miniatures use detail strategically rather than covering every surface with texture. — [Sculpey blog, Adding Tiny Details That Make Polymer Clay Miniatures Feel Believable](https://www.sculpey.com/blogs/blog/adding-tiny-details-that-make-polymer-clay-miniatures-feel-believable)

### Inferences
- **Translating heroic conventions into numbers for a figurine.** Sourced: heads "almost interchangeable" across a 1:48 vs 1:56 height gap, i.e. roughly 15% oversize. Applying that to the canon table above gives a heroic 28-32 mm-style build of roughly 6-7 heads tall, rather than 8. This is my calculation, not a published canon.
- **Exaggeration list for a 30-60 mm FDM figurine**, synthesizing the cited conventions:
  - Head up about 10-20%.
  - Hands and feet thicker; keep hand ≥ face length.
  - Shoulders broader.
  - Any held object chunkier.
  - Eye sockets, the under-brow shelf, the nostril/mouth line and under-chin shadows cut deeper than true anatomy, so they cast shadow after printing and painting.
- Big forms first: the cited practice (socket before eyeball, detail used strategically, small details won't capture) supports blocking masses and planes, then adding only the details that survive at final scale. At FDM resolution a 0.4 mm nozzle sets that bar much higher than resin miniatures, which is where these heroic conventions came from.

### Gaps
- No Games Workshop sculptor interview or Putty&Paint article with explicit numeric head/hand oversize ratios was found. A "6 heads tall heroic" figure is folklore I could not confirm.
- No ZBrush figurine tutorial with numeric exaggeration guidance was fetched.

## 4. Animal anatomy simplification (cats, dogs, birds)

### Takeaway
Build quadrupeds as two masses, ribcage and pelvis, joined by a flexible spine. The ribcage is about half the torso length and is usually drawn too small. The quadruped pelvis tilts strongly forward. The hind leg must have a "Z" shape: the backward-pointing joint halfway up is the hock (ankle), not a knee. Straight legs give a "table" look.

### Cited Findings
- Treat the body as two masses, ribcage and pelvis, joined by a flexible spine. A sausage-shaped torso wrapping both masses, with a slight dip along the spine, is a common simplification. The ribcage occupies about half the length of the torso and is often drawn too small. A quadruped's pelvis has a large forward lean, unlike a human's nearly vertical pelvis. — search summary of [Drawabox, Lesson 5: Animal Constructions](https://www.drawabox.com/lesson/5/2) (the page itself returned HTTP 503 on fetch; these points came from search snippets)
- The joint mistaken for a "backward knee" on the hind leg is the hock, roughly halfway up the leg. The tarsus protrudes backward. The hind leg should have a distinct "Z" shape, and four straight legs produce a "table" look. — search summary of [Drawabox, Lesson 5](https://www.drawabox.com/lesson/5/2) and [3D World, Animal Anatomy (Eaton), via Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/doc/244404336/3dWorld-Animal-Anatomy-Eaton)
- Suggested build order: spine, then skull, ribcage, pelvis, tail, then muscle and outline. — [Udemy, Masterclass of Animal Drawing](https://www.udemy.com/course/masterclass-of-animal-drawing-beginner-to-advanced/)

### Inferences
- For a cat/dog figurine: block the cranium + muzzle, the ribcage (about half the torso length, deep chest), and a smaller forward-tilted pelvis. Connect them along a spine curve that carries the gesture. Then give every leg a zig-zag: on the front leg, elbow behind, wrist forward, paw; on the hind leg, knee forward, hock back, paw. The "Z" is the single most important cue for realism.
- Cats and dogs stand on their toes (digitigrade), so the hock is the anatomical heel. This is consistent with the hock = ankle finding.

### Gaps
- No detailed, reliable source was fetched for dog-specific masses (breed head shapes, stop between skull and muzzle) or cat head construction. Drawabox's lesson (the best candidate) was unavailable (503).
- No bird-body (non-wing) mass simplification was sourced, e.g. egg-shaped body, neck S-curve hidden in feathers, leg position.

## 5. Hands and feet simplified; how limbs bend

### Takeaway
The only rules verified this session are length checks: hand ≈ face (chin to hairline), foot ≈ forearm (some say ≈ one head), and wrist level with the crotch. Mitten and foot-wedge simplification and the knee/elbow bend rules were not confirmed from a fetched source. The animal Z-leg rule (section 4) covers quadrupeds.

### Cited Findings
- Hand length (base of palm to middle fingertip) ≈ face length (chin to hairline). — [scienceinsights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-proportion-a-body-for-figure-drawing/); [Wikipedia, Body proportions](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Body_proportion)
- Foot ≈ forearm (inside elbow crease to wrist); other sources say ≈ one head length. — [scienceinsights](https://scienceinsights.org/how-to-proportion-a-body-for-figure-drawing/)
- Wrist aligns with the crotch / hip-joint line; the elbow aligns with the waist / bottom of the ribcage. — [Fiveable](https://fiveable.me/drawing-foundations/unit-10/proportions-human-body/study-guide/bVYcw96jRRpCUZJk)
- Heroic miniatures thicken hands and feet. — [TabletopXtra](https://tabletopxtra.co.uk/blogs/news/28mm-vs-32mm-vs-35mm-a-heroic-scale-buyers-guide-for-3d-printed-miniatures)

### Inferences
- At 60 mm figure height with an 8-head canon, the face is about 5.6 mm (chin to hairline is about ¾ of head height). The hand is therefore about 5.6 mm long, and individual fingers are well under 1 mm thick. On FDM this argues for mitten hands (fingers fused into a block, thumb separate) at anything under roughly 100 mm.

### Gaps (commonly taught, NOT verified with a source this session; the report writer should not state these as cited facts)
- The mitten hand (palm block + finger block + thumb wedge, knuckle arc not straight) and the wedge/doorstop foot (high inner arch, low outer edge, heel block) are standard teaching in Loomis/Bridgman/Hampton, but no fetched source confirmed them.
- Knee and elbow bend direction and the "foot points slightly outward" rule were not sourced.

## 6. Wings (bird and angel): feather groups and silhouette

### Takeaway
A wing is an arm. The primaries (usually 10, long and pointed) attach to the hand bones. The secondaries (6 to over 40 depending on species, rounder tips) attach to the ulna. The upper arm carries no flight feathers, only tertials and scapulars near the body. Coverts layer over the flight-feather bases in rows: lesser, median, greater. The alula sits on the thumb. When folded, the primaries tuck under the secondaries. Build from top layers (marginal coverts, alula) down to the flight feathers.

### Cited Findings
- Wing skeleton: humerus, radius/ulna, carpals (wrist), metacarpals and phalanges (hand). Primaries attach to the hand bones and secondaries to the ulna. "No feathers attach directly to the upper arm." A ligament from shoulder to wrist gives the wing its curved leading edge. Alula feathers are controlled by the thumb. — [Clip Studio Tips, Feathered Wings: A Drawing Tutorial](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/7950)
- Counts: "Most birds have ten primary feathers on each wing, though that can vary." Secondaries number 6 to over 40 by species. — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/7950)
- Covert layering: greater coverts form a second row over the gaps, on both surfaces. Median coverts cover the secondaries on top, and lesser coverts fill the rest. Scapulars cover the shoulders. Tertials close the gap between secondaries and body. Axillaries sit in the underwing "armpit." — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/7950); [Birds Outside My Window, Anatomy: wing coverts](https://www.birdsoutsidemywindow.org/2010/01/02/anatomy-wing-coverts/)
- Overlap direction: on the underside the outermost feathers are on top; on the top side the feathers closest to the body are on top. — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/7950)
- Primaries are more pointed and secondaries rounder at the tip. When a wing folds, the primaries go underneath the secondaries. — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/7950)
- Recommended drawing order: top layers (marginal coverts, alula) first, lowest layers (primaries and secondaries) last. Feather number and shape vary by species, so use reference. — [Clip Studio Tips](https://tips.clip-studio.com/en-us/articles/7950)
- The alula acts like a leading-edge slat, preventing stall at low speed. — [Birds Outside My Window](https://www.birdsoutsidemywindow.org/2010/01/02/anatomy-wing-coverts/)
- Labelled external-anatomy diagram of bird parts, useful as a quick reference. — [BirdsCaribbean, Parts of a Bird (Christine Elder, PDF)](https://www.birdscaribbean.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Parts-of-a-bird-Christine-Elder.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Believable wing silhouette for a sculpt.** The leading edge is an arm: shoulder → elbow (pointing back) → wrist (pointing forward, the wing's "corner") → hand. The trailing edge is a fan of 10 primaries off the hand, the outermost longest and most pointed, plus a scalloped row of rounder secondaries off the forearm. The covert rows read as 2-3 bands of shorter, overlapping scales. Getting the forward-pointing wrist corner and the long primary fan right is what reads as "wing" in a thumbnail. Getting the covert banding right is what reads as "realistic" up close.
- For printing, model the flight feathers as overlapping slabs, with each feather's leading edge stepped proud of the next, rather than as individually separated feathers. The overlap-direction rule above tells you which edge steps up.
- For angel wings on a human figure, the cited bird anatomy implies that the wing root (shoulder) needs a mass on the back above the shoulder blades. Its forward-pointing wrist corner should land near or above head height for an open wing.

### Gaps
- No source on angel-wing attachment to a human back or wing span relative to body height was found. The fetched Clip Studio article explicitly does not cover it.
- No sourced numeric ratio for wing segment lengths (humerus : forearm : hand).
